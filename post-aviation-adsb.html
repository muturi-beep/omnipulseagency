ADS-B Movement Intelligence: Beyond the Flight Radar

How structured ADS-B data reveals fleet utilisation patterns, sanctioned-country overflights, and operator behaviours that raw flight-trackers miss — with real-world case examples.

By Aviation Intelligence Team • 7 min read

If you have ever tracked a flight on a public website like Flightradar24, you have interacted with ADS-B data. Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) is the technology that allows aircraft to broadcast their identity, position, altitude, and velocity to ground stations and other aircraft.

For the general public, it is a novelty. But for aviation lessors, insurers, compliance officers, and intelligence analysts, ADS-B is a goldmine of operational intelligence. The challenge is that raw ADS-B data — millions of pings per day — is noise. To extract value, it must be structured, cleaned, and analysed.

This is what we call Movement Intelligence. Here is how it goes beyond the flight radar.

1. Fleet Utilisation & Asset Productivity

A flight radar will show you where a plane is right now. Movement Intelligence tells you how that aircraft has been utilised over the last 12 months. For an aviation lessor or financier, this is critical.

What we analyse:

  • Flight hours vs. ground time: Is the asset underutilised or overworked?
  • Route concentration: Is the aircraft flying the same short-haul routes repeatedly (high cycles, lower value) or long-haul routes (higher value)?
  • Operator changes: Has the aircraft been transferred between operators without the lessor's knowledge?

This data helps financiers assess whether an asset is being maintained according to the lease agreement and whether its market value is holding up.

2. Sanctions & Sanctioned-Country Overflights

Compliance is the most high-stakes application of ADS-B data. If an aircraft flies into, out of, or even *over* a sanctioned jurisdiction, it can trigger a regulatory red flag.

What we analyse:

  • Airspace violations: Tracking whether the aircraft entered the airspace of a sanctioned country (e.g., Iran, North Korea, Syria).
  • Destination anomalies: A flight that disappears from radar and reappears in a sanctioned country is a massive red flag.
  • Transponder manipulation: Some operators deliberately turn off their ADS-B transponder to hide their movements. We identify gaps in data that suggest deliberate "dark" flying.

ADS-B data is not just about where a plane is. It is about where a plane has been. A pattern of overflights or landings in sanctioned territories can expose a lessor or insurer to significant legal and reputational risk, even if the aircraft is currently in a compliant jurisdiction.

3. Operator Behaviour & Risk Profiling

Aircraft registries tell you who owns the plane. ADS-B tells you how they fly it. The behaviour of an operator is often a leading indicator of risk.

What we analyse:

  • Route discipline: Does the operator consistently fly safe, approved routes, or do they frequently deviate?
  • Altitude and speed anomalies: Unusual flight profiles can indicate technical issues, pilot error, or illicit activity.
  • Cross-referencing with registries: If the operator flying the aircraft is not the registered operator, that is a red flag for unauthorised sub-leasing or wet-leasing.

This behavioural data is invaluable for insurers assessing the risk profile of a new client, or for lessors ensuring their assets are being operated in a safe and compliant manner.

4. Applied AI & Data Readiness

Raw ADS-B data is messy. It contains duplicate pings, missing data points, and false signals. To build a reliable Movement Intelligence platform, the data must be cleaned, structured, and prepared for machine learning models.
