Aircraft Lessor Structures: Tracing Ownership Through SPVs

Special purpose vehicles, multi-jurisdiction holding arrangements, and beneficial ownership — how we map the true controllers of commercial aircraft for lessors and insurers.

By Aviation Team • 8 min read

In the aviation industry, the ownership of a commercial aircraft is rarely straightforward. Unlike a car or a house, an aircraft is a high-value, mobile asset that is typically owned by a special purpose vehicle (SPV) and leased to an operator. This structure allows for efficient financing, risk management, and tax planning.

But it also creates a layer of opacity that can be exploited by bad actors. For lessors, insurers, and compliance teams, understanding the true ownership structure is essential for assessing risk.

Here is how we trace aircraft ownership through SPVs and multi-jurisdiction holding arrangements.

1. What is a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV)?

An SPV is a legal entity created for a specific, narrow purpose — in this case, holding title to a single aircraft. The SPV is typically a subsidiary of a larger leasing company or financial institution. It is designed to isolate the asset from the parent company's financial risks.

Why SPVs are used:

  • Asset isolation: If the parent company goes bankrupt, the aircraft is protected from creditors.
  • Financing efficiency: SPVs make it easier to secure financing for a specific aircraft.
  • Tax optimization: Different jurisdictions offer different tax treatment for aviation assets.

2. The Multi-Jurisdiction Challenge

A single aircraft can be owned by an SPV registered in Ireland, financed by a bank in Singapore, leased to an airline in the Middle East, and insured by a syndicate in London. This multi-jurisdiction structure is common and legitimate, but it makes ownership tracing complex.

What we look for:

  • Chain of incorporation: We trace the SPV back through its parent companies, identifying the ultimate beneficial owner (UBO).
  • Registry filings: We cross-reference public records from the FAA, EASA, ICAO, and national registries to verify the ownership chain.
  • UBO thresholds: Different jurisdictions have different thresholds for declaring a UBO. We identify the true controllers, not just the legal owners.

A compliant SPV is not necessarily a safe asset. If the UBO of the SPV is a sanctioned individual or entity, the aircraft can still be subject to enforcement action. Tracing the full chain of ownership is the only way to mitigate this risk.

3. Why This Matters for Lessors and Insurers

The consequences of failing to trace ownership can be severe.

For Lessors: If you lease an aircraft to an operator that is controlled by a sanctioned entity, you could be in breach of sanctions regulations. You may also be exposed to reputational damage and the loss of the asset.

For Insurers: If you insure an aircraft that is later found to be controlled by a sanctioned entity, you may be unable to enforce the policy. In some cases, the insurer may be held liable for sanctions violations.
