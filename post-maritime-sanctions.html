Sanctions Screening in Maritime: A Practical Guide for Compliance Teams

Navigating OFAC, EU, and UN regimes — and why list-matching alone is not enough. We show how structured vessel ownership data transforms compliance workflows.

By Maritime Team • 10 min read

Maritime sanctions compliance is one of the most complex and high-stakes areas of regulatory risk. A single vessel trading with a sanctioned entity can expose a shipowner, charterer, or financier to millions of dollars in fines, reputational damage, and even criminal liability.

The challenge is that the maritime industry is inherently opaque. Vessels change hands frequently, ownership structures are deliberately complex, and sanctioned entities are increasingly sophisticated at hiding their involvement.

This guide is designed for compliance teams who need to move beyond basic list-matching and build a more robust, data-driven screening process.

1. The Three Major Sanctions Regimes

If you are operating in the maritime sector, you need to be aware of the three primary sanctions regimes:

  • OFAC (Office of Foreign Assets Control): The US Treasury department's sanctions arm. OFAC sanctions are the most aggressively enforced and carry the highest penalties.
  • EU Sanctions: The European Union maintains its own sanctions list, which often overlaps with OFAC but can differ in scope and target.
  • UN Sanctions: The United Nations Security Council imposes sanctions that are binding on all member states.

A robust compliance program screens against all three regimes, as well as any relevant national lists (such as the UK's OFSI).

2. Why List-Matching is Not Enough

The most common mistake in maritime compliance is relying solely on list-matching. This means checking a vessel's name, IMO number, or registered owner against a sanctions list. While this is a necessary first step, it is not sufficient.

Why it fails:

  • Sanctioned entities can hide behind compliant registered owners: A vessel may be owned by a clean SPV, but if the beneficial owner is sanctioned, the vessel is still a sanctioned asset.
  • Vessels change names: A vessel can be renamed to evade detection. The IMO number is the only permanent identifier, but even that can be manipulated.
  • Dark fleet tactics: Sanctioned actors use AIS manipulation, ship-to-ship transfers, and falsified documentation to hide their activities.

The key to effective sanctions screening is not just checking names, but understanding the full ownership and operational structure of a vessel. If you do not know who the beneficial owner, technical manager, and commercial controller are, you cannot accurately assess the risk.

3. Building a Data-Driven Screening Workflow

A modern maritime compliance workflow should include the following steps:

  • Step 1: Identify all seven roles of ownership: Beneficial owner, registered owner, ISM manager, technical manager, commercial manager, commercial controller, and DOC holder.
  • Step 2: Screen each role against all relevant sanctions lists: Do not just screen the registered owner.
  • Step 3: Analyze AIS movement data: Look for patterns of sanctioned-country overflights, AIS gaps, or ship-to-ship transfers.
  • Step 4: Document your findings: A clear, auditable record of your screening process is essential for demonstrating compliance to regulators.

4. The Cost of Non-Compliance

The penalties for maritime sanctions violations are severe. OFAC has fined companies millions of dollars for sanctions breaches, and in some cases, individuals have faced criminal prosecution. Beyond the financial penalties, the reputational damage can be catastrophic, leading to loss of banking relationships, insurance coverage, and customer trust.
